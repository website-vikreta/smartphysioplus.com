If you have never seen a physiotherapist, the first visit can feel like a mystery. You may wonder whether you will be asked to exercise straight away, whether it will hurt, or whether you need a scan before you come. This guide explains what usually happens, so you can arrive prepared. For how we work day to day, see [physiotherapy at Smart Physio+](/physiotherapy/).

## Why the first visit is an assessment, not a treatment

Pain is a signal, not a diagnosis. Back pain, for example, can come from muscles, joints, discs, nerves or posture, and sometimes from somewhere else in the body. Treating without finding the likely source is guesswork. That is why a good first visit is mostly about listening, looking and testing, so the plan that follows fits you.

Some hands-on treatment or advice may start in the first visit, but only if the assessment supports it. You should leave knowing what the physiotherapist found, what she thinks is going on, and what the next steps are.

## Before you come

A little preparation makes the visit more useful.

- **Bring your reports.** Scans, X-rays, blood tests, discharge summaries and prescriptions all help. Do not worry if you have none. Many people do not need a scan before a first visit.
- **Write down the key facts.** When did the problem start? What makes it better or worse? What have you already tried? Have you had it before?
- **List your medicines** and any other health conditions, such as diabetes, high blood pressure or a heart condition.
- **Wear loose, comfortable clothes** that let you move freely and let the physiotherapist see the area, such as shorts for a knee or a loose top for a shoulder.
- **Think about your goals.** Do you want to sit through a workday, climb stairs, return to a sport or sleep through the night? Goals shape the plan.

## What happens during the assessment

Every clinic has its own order, but most assessments follow the same pattern.

### 1. A conversation

You will be asked about your pain: where it is, what it feels like, when it started, and whether it spreads. You will be asked about your daily routine, your work, your sleep and any sport. You will be asked about past injuries, operations and medical history. This part can take a while, and it is the most important part. Answer as fully as you can, including things that seem unrelated.

### 2. A review of your reports

If you bring scans or reports, the physiotherapist reads them. A report is one piece of information. It does not always match how the problem feels, and many people have changes on a scan with no pain at all. The examination tells her how the problem behaves in your body.

### 3. A physical examination

You will be watched as you stand, walk, bend, squat or reach, depending on the problem. The physiotherapist may check your posture, how far a joint moves, how strong the surrounding muscles are, how your nerves respond, and where it is tender. She may ask you to repeat a movement to see what changes your pain. Tell her when something hurts. The aim is to find the problem, not to push through pain.

### 4. An explanation

After the examination, you should hear in plain words what she found and what it means. This is a good time to ask questions. If something needs a doctor's opinion first, she should say so.

### 5. A plan

The plan describes what treatment may include, how often you might need to come, and what you can do at home. A plan is a starting point. It changes as you improve, or if something is not working.

## Questions worth asking

- What do you think is causing my pain?
- Is there anything I should stop doing, and anything I should keep doing?
- How many visits might I need, and how will we know I am improving?
- What can I do at home between visits?
- Is there anything that makes you think I should see a doctor?

## Will it hurt?

Some tests and techniques can feel uncomfortable, especially on a sore area. They should not cause sharp pain. If something hurts more than you can tolerate, say so and the physiotherapist can change what she is doing. You are in control of the visit.

- sudden weakness in an arm or leg, or a drooping face
- chest pain, or breathlessness that comes on suddenly
- loss of control of your bladder or bowel, or numbness around the groin or inner thighs
- severe pain after a fall or accident
- fever with a hot, swollen joint
- pain that is getting steadily worse and wakes you at night with no clear cause

These can need urgent medical care, so do not wait for a physiotherapy appointment. Go to a hospital or call a doctor.

## What comes after the first visit

Most plans combine hands-on treatment, exercises and advice. Over the next few visits the physiotherapist checks how you respond and adjusts the plan. Home exercises are usually a large part of recovery, because the work you do between visits often matters as much as the visit itself.

You are always free to ask why something is being done. A good plan makes sense to you.
