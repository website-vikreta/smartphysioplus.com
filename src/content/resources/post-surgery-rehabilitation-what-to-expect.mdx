An operation fixes or replaces a part of the body. Rehabilitation is how you get movement, strength and confidence back afterwards. The two belong together. This guide explains what recovery usually involves, so you know what to expect and what to ask. For how we support people after surgery, see our page on <PageLink slug="post-surgery-rehabilitation">post-surgery rehabilitation</PageLink>.

## Your surgeon sets the rules

Every operation is different, and so is every person. How soon you can bear weight, how far a joint may bend, and what you should avoid in the first weeks are decisions your surgeon makes, based on what was done and how it went. A rehabilitation plan follows those instructions. It does not replace them.

If anything in this guide differs from what your surgeon told you, follow your surgeon. Bring your discharge summary and any written instructions to your first physiotherapy visit so everyone works from the same plan.

## Why rehabilitation matters

After an operation, the muscles around the area switch off quickly and weaken. Joints stiffen if they are not moved. Swelling and pain make people protect the area, and this can lead to habits such as limping that last long after the wound heals. Planned exercise, started at the right time and built up step by step, works against each of these problems.

Rehabilitation is not only about the operated area. Time in bed or on a reduced routine affects the whole body: general strength, balance and stamina. A good plan brings these back too.

## The usual stages

The timing differs between operations, but recovery usually moves through stages like these.

### Early recovery

In hospital or in the first days at home, the focus is on safety and basic movement. This may include breathing exercises, gentle movement of nearby joints, getting in and out of bed, and learning to walk with an aid if you need one. Pain and swelling are managed. You will be told what to avoid.

### Restoring movement

As the area settles, the aim is to regain range of movement and reduce stiffness. Exercises are gentle and often repeated several times a day. A physiotherapist checks that you do them correctly, because doing them wrongly can slow you down.

### Building strength

Once movement returns, the focus moves to strength in the muscles that support the area, then in the legs, hips, back or arms as a whole. Exercises become harder step by step. Progress is checked before each step is added.

### Returning to normal life

Later stages work on balance, walking pattern, stairs, getting up from a low seat, and the specific demands of your work or hobbies. For some people this means returning to sport. For others it means managing stairs and shopping safely. Goals are set with you.

## What a rehabilitation visit may include

A visit may include an assessment of how the area is healing, measurements such as how far a joint bends, hands-on techniques to ease stiffness, supervised exercises, advice on daily activities, and a home programme. At each visit the plan can be adjusted. Home exercises usually matter as much as visits, because recovery depends on what you do every day.

## What is normal and what is not

Some pain, swelling and tiredness are normal in the weeks after surgery. Swelling may be worse at the end of the day. Stiffness is common in the morning. Progress is rarely a straight line: some days feel better than others.

<StopBox slug="post-surgery-rehabilitation-what-to-expect">

- a wound that is hot, red, leaking or opening
- fever or chills
- swelling that suddenly increases, or a calf that is swollen, warm and painful
- breathlessness or chest pain
- new numbness, loss of colour or coldness in the limb
- pain that suddenly becomes much worse, or a sudden fall or twist of the operated area

Contact your surgeon or hospital straight away. For chest pain or breathlessness, get emergency care.

</StopBox>

## How to prepare

- Bring your discharge summary, surgeon's notes, scan reports and a list of your medicines to the first visit.
- Wear loose clothing that lets the physiotherapist see the area.
- Think about practical needs at home: stairs, bathroom, a firm chair, who can help in the first weeks.
- Write down questions for your surgeon and your physiotherapist.
- Be realistic. Recovery takes weeks to months, and the timeline depends on the operation and on you.

## Questions worth asking

- What movements or loads should I avoid, and for how long?
- When can I start exercises, and which ones?
- When can I walk without an aid, drive, climb stairs or return to work?
- What signs mean I should call you?
- When will I be checked again?
