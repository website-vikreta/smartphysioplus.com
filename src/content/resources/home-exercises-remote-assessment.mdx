Many people ask whether physiotherapy can be done from home, over a call or a video. Home exercises are a normal and valuable part of recovery. Remote advice has its uses. It also has limits. This guide explains what can be judged from a distance, what cannot, and how to do home exercises safely. If you think an in-person assessment would be better, you can [request an appointment](/book-appointment/).

## Home exercises are part of most plans

Almost every physiotherapy plan includes exercises to do at home. The reason is simple: a visit might last under an hour, but your recovery depends on what you do the rest of the week. Regular, well-chosen exercises help you rebuild strength and movement, and they teach you how to manage your own problem.

Exercises work best when they are chosen for you, shown to you, and checked. A generic list from the internet may suit someone else's problem, not yours.

## What can often be judged remotely

A video call or a set of photos and clips can help with some things.

- **Checking a movement you already know.** A physiotherapist can watch you do an exercise and correct how you do it, such as your posture, speed or range.
- **General advice.** Guidance on daily habits, desk set-up, pacing activity or sleeping positions can often be given by call.
- **Progress checks.** Between in-person visits, a short call can review how you are doing and adjust a home programme.
- **Planning.** Talking through your history, symptoms and goals is possible by call, and it can help you decide what to do next.

## What cannot be judged well remotely

Some things need a physiotherapist in the room with you.

- **A first assessment of a new or unclear problem.** Many findings come from touch and measured testing: how tender an area is, how a joint moves when it is moved for you, how strong a muscle is when resisted, how a nerve responds.
- **Anything that needs hands-on treatment.** Manual therapy cannot be delivered over a screen.
- **Problems where something serious might be hiding.** A camera cannot tell whether swelling is from a sprain or something that needs urgent care.
- **Fine movement problems.** Balance, coordination and gait are hard to judge on a small screen, and safety matters if you are unsteady.
- **Machine-based treatments.** Equipment such as robotic spine decompression, shockwave, TECAR, laser and electromagnetic therapies are clinic-only.

So a remote conversation can support care, but it does not replace a proper first assessment for most problems.

## How to do home exercises safely

- **Start from an assessment.** Exercises chosen after an examination are more likely to suit you.
- **Learn each exercise in person first**, then repeat it at home. Ask for the key points in writing or on a short video.
- **Do them as shown.** More is not better. Pace and number matter.
- **Expect mild effort or mild discomfort, not sharp pain.** If an exercise causes sharp pain, stop and tell your physiotherapist.
- **Keep a short log.** Note what you did, how it felt and any change. It helps at the next visit.
- **Build up gradually.** Increase only when the current level feels comfortable, or when your physiotherapist advises.
- **Make them part of your routine.** Pair them with something you already do, like after brushing your teeth.

- sharp or severe pain during or after an exercise that does not settle
- new numbness, tingling or weakness
- dizziness, chest pain or breathlessness while exercising
- swelling that appears suddenly, or a hot, red joint
- a fall or injury while exercising

Stop the exercise. For chest pain, breathlessness, or sudden weakness, get emergency care. For other symptoms, contact a doctor or your physiotherapist before you continue.

## What to do if you cannot travel

Some people find it hard to travel: after surgery, with limited mobility, or when unwell. If that is you, tell the clinic when you book. We can discuss what is possible. A single in-person assessment, followed by a home programme and later check-ins, is sometimes a practical way to combine both.

## Choosing between remote and in person

Remote advice may be reasonable if:

- you already had an in-person assessment and need to check your exercises
- your problem is stable and well understood
- you need general advice rather than a diagnosis

An in-person visit is the better choice if:

- the problem is new, getting worse or unclear
- you want hands-on treatment
- you have had surgery or an injury
- you feel unsteady, or you have symptoms that worry you

## Questions to ask about your home programme

- How many times a day or week should I do each exercise, and for how long?
- Which exercises should I stop if my pain increases, and by how much is too much?
- How will I know I am ready to make an exercise harder?
- Can you show me, or write down, the key points for each exercise?
- When should I come back for a check, and what should I bring to it?

Clear answers to these make it far easier to keep going at home, and to notice early if something is not working.
