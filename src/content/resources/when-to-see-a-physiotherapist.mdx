Most aches settle by themselves. A stiff back after a long drive, a sore knee after a new workout, a shoulder that complains after painting a wall: these often ease within days. The difficult part is knowing when to wait and when to get help. This guide gives practical signs for the three most common problem areas. If your pain is in the lower back, see our page on <PageLink slug="back-pain-physiotherapy">back pain physiotherapy</PageLink>.

## Start with the safety check

Some symptoms need a doctor, not a physiotherapist, and they need one soon. Check the box below first. If none apply, the rest of this guide can help you decide.

<StopBox slug="when-to-see-a-physiotherapist">

- pain after a fall, accident or sports injury with swelling, deformity, or inability to use the limb
- fever with a hot, swollen or very painful joint
- sudden weakness, numbness or a drooping face
- loss of control of your bladder or bowel, or numbness around the groin, with back pain
- chest pain, or a swollen, painful calf with breathlessness
- severe pain at night that nothing eases, especially if you have had cancer or are unexpectedly losing weight

These can be serious. Go to a hospital or see a doctor without delay.

</StopBox>

## Signs it is worth an assessment

Whatever the area, these signs suggest that a physiotherapy assessment is a good next step.

- The pain has lasted more than a couple of weeks without clearly improving.
- It keeps coming back each time you do the same activity.
- It limits things you want to do: sitting through a workday, climbing stairs, sleeping, lifting your arm.
- You have changed how you move to avoid pain, such as limping or holding your arm close.
- You are not sure what is causing it, and you do not want to guess.
- You are returning to a sport or job after injury and want a safe plan.

You do not need to wait for the pain to be severe. Early advice is often simpler than late advice.

## Back pain

Back pain is very common and often improves with gentle movement. An assessment is worth considering if:

- pain has lasted more than two weeks, or keeps returning
- pain spreads into the buttock or leg, or you feel tingling or numbness
- sitting, bending or lifting is getting harder
- you have a disc problem on a scan and want to understand what it means for you

Back pain with leg weakness that is getting worse, or any change in bladder or bowel control, needs a doctor straight away.

## Knee pain

For knees, think about what the knee is doing as well as how it feels. An assessment is worth considering if:

- the knee hurts on stairs, squatting or after sport, and this is not settling
- it feels stiff or swollen after activity
- it clicks with pain, catches or feels unstable
- you want to keep running or playing but the pain returns when you build up

A knee that is hot, red and swollen, that locks so you cannot straighten it, or that cannot take weight after a twist or fall needs a doctor first. ## Shoulder pain

Shoulders are complicated and often hurt from the neck or upper back as well. An assessment is worth considering if:

- reaching overhead, behind your back or across your body hurts
- pain is worse at night or when lying on that side
- the shoulder feels weak, or you are losing movement
- pain started after a fall, lifting or repeated overhead work and is not settling

Sudden loss of movement or strength after an injury needs a doctor. Our page on <PageLink slug="shoulder-pain-physiotherapy">shoulder pain physiotherapy</PageLink> covers what an assessment looks at.

## Do I need a scan first?

Usually not. Many people are assessed first, and a physiotherapist will tell you if a scan or a doctor's opinion would help. If you already have scans or reports, bring them. They are one piece of the picture, not the whole answer.

## What to do while you decide

- Keep moving gently within what is comfortable.
- Avoid the single activity that sharply worsens the pain, but do not stop everything.
- Note when the pain is better and worse. This is very helpful at an assessment.
- Do not rely on painkillers alone to keep going. If you need them regularly, get it checked.

## What an assessment will tell you

At an assessment you should hear what the physiotherapist thinks is causing the problem, whether physiotherapy is likely to help, and what a plan could look like. Sometimes the advice is simple: a few changes and exercises. Sometimes it is a referral to a doctor. Either way, you leave with a clearer picture than you came with.
