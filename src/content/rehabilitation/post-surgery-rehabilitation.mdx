## Why rehabilitation matters after surgery

An operation repairs or replaces a part of the body. Rehabilitation is how you get your movement, strength and confidence back afterwards. The surgery and the rehabilitation are two halves of the same treatment.

After an operation, muscles near the area switch off and weaken quickly. Joints stiffen if they are not moved. Swelling and pain make people protect the area, which can lead to new habits such as limping. Planned exercise, started at the right time and built up step by step, works against each of these.

### Knee replacement

Knee replacement is one of the most common operations we help people recover from. In the early weeks, the main goals are to get the knee straight, bend it a little more each week, wake up the thigh muscle and walk safely, first with a frame or sticks and then without. Later, the work turns to stairs, getting up from the floor, longer walks and the activities you had the operation for in the first place.

If you are still deciding about surgery, our page on <PageLink slug="knee-pain-physiotherapy">knee pain physiotherapy</PageLink> explains how we look after painful knees before any operation.

### Other joint and spine surgery

We also see people after other joint operations, such as shoulder, hip or ankle surgery, and after some spine operations. Each operation and each surgeon has a different protocol: what you may move, how much weight you may take, and when. We follow that protocol closely and ask your surgeon when anything is unclear.

## When to start, and working with your surgeon

Your surgeon sets the rules for the first weeks. These may cover how much weight you can put through the leg, which movements to avoid, how long to wear a brace, and when stitches or staples come out. We do not change those rules. We help you get the most from them and keep your surgeon informed when needed.

Contact your surgeon or hospital straight away, rather than waiting for physiotherapy, if you notice:

<RedFlags slug="post-surgery-rehabilitation">

- the wound becomes red, hot, more painful or starts to leak
- a fever, chills or feeling generally unwell
- a swollen, tight or painful calf, or sudden shortness of breath or chest pain
- a sudden increase in pain or swelling, or a clunk or giving way in the joint
- new numbness or weakness in the leg or arm after spine surgery

Chest pain or breathlessness after surgery can mean a clot in the lung. Call an ambulance.

## How Smart Physio+ approaches post-surgery rehabilitation

**Before the first session.** When you book, tell us the operation, the date and your surgeon's name. Bring your paperwork to the first visit.

**Assessment.** Dr. Nileema Chaudhary reads your discharge summary and the surgeon's instructions. She checks your wound from the outside, measures how far the joint moves, tests the strength of the muscles around it and watches you walk and get up from a chair. She asks what your days look like at home: stairs, the bathroom, a two-wheeler, work.

**Treatment.** Your plan may include:

- exercises matched to your surgeon's protocol, changed as each stage is reached
- gentle hands-on treatment to help movement and ease tight tissue around the operated area
- walking practice, with aids, and then without them when it is safe
- balance and stair practice
- advice on swelling, sleep positions and getting in and out of a car

**Later stages.** As healing allows, the work shifts to strength, endurance and returning to work, travel or sport. We agree on goals that matter to you, such as sitting cross-legged, a morning walk, or a game with your grandchildren, and plan towards them within what your surgeon allows.

## What to bring and what to expect

Bring:

- your discharge summary
- your surgeon's notes or exercise sheet
- any X-rays taken after the operation
- a list of your current medicines
- loose clothing that lets us see the operated area

The first visit is unhurried. You leave knowing what to do at home until the next session. At each visit we measure movement and strength again so you can see change. Where your surgeon has follow-up appointments, we can give you a short note on your progress to take along.
